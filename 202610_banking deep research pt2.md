# Spanish Banking Evolution, 1920–2026: Historical Extension to the Consolidation Map

## Evidence base, scope and what Part Two changes

This research extends the original **1988–2026 Spanish banking consolidation project** into a century-scale history running from **1920 to October 27, 2026**, while retaining earlier founding dates when they are necessary to explain the ancestry of today's banking groups. It preserves the original project's key distinction between legal mergers, absorptions, acquisitions, SIPs, banking-business transfers, resolutions, asset transfers, rebrandings, minority investments and failed transactions. fileciteturn0file0 fileciteturn0file1

The most important methodological change is that the website should no longer be thought of as a single M&A graph. It needs **three connected layers**:

| Layer | What belongs in it | What must not be inferred |
|---|---|---|
| Institutions | Banks, cajas, bank subsidiaries, banking foundations where historically necessary, and current destination groups | A current bank is not automatically the same legal person as every predecessor |
| Institutional relationships | Merger, absorption, SIP, business transfer, acquisition, resolution sale, rebranding, minority stake, failed bid | A minority stake or failed offer cannot create ancestry |
| Historical context | Laws, banking crises, European integration, FROB, the 2012 MoU, SAREB, supervisory reforms | A law or crisis is not an edge between two banks |

That separation is essential. A user should be able to see, for example, that the **1921 Banking Law changed the rules governing the whole system** without the visualization fabricating a relationship between Banco de España and a commercial bank.

### The two sources in the supplied image have been incorporated as core sources

The first image-specified source, Banco de España's **_Guía de archivos históricos de la banca en España_**, published in 2019, is considerably more valuable than a normal archive directory. Banco de España explains that it identifies 180 historical banking archives held by ten institutions or foundations, and the volume contains both Pedro Tedde's study of twentieth-century Spanish banking and María del Carmen Angulo Teja's historical **“Mapa de bancos y cajas de ahorros.”** citeturn0search0turn2view0

**Research-quality conclusion:** the historical extension is strongest when the 2019 map is used as a **genealogical index**, the 2022 study and Banco de España historical research are used for **system history**, and individual transactions are then checked against Banco de España, FROB, BOE, CNMV, SRB/ECB and bank corporate records rather than being inferred from the map alone. ## A century of structural evolution

The central historical finding is that Spanish banking did not move in a straight line from “many banks” to “few banks.” It passed through several distinct regimes: rapid interwar financial deepening; wartime fragmentation and post-war state control; developmental-era modernization; crisis-driven restructuring and liberalization; European-market consolidation; the destruction and reconstruction of the caja model; and finally a concentrated, technologically transformed industry in which ownership transactions can occur without consolidation.

| Era | What changed | Three dominant forces |
|---|---|---|
| **1920–1935** | Rapid banking growth accompanied by early modern regulation and increasing concentration | Financial deepening; 1921 regulatory reform; growth of large nationwide banks |
| **1936–1959** | War disruption followed by an interventionist, protected banking system | Civil War; autarky/state-directed credit; 1946 banking status quo |
| **1960–1977** | Modernization after the Stabilization Plan; expansion of banks and cajas | Economic growth; 1962 reform; branch and balance-sheet expansion |
| **1978–1987** | Banking crisis and rescues alongside progressive liberalization | Bank failures; deregulation; EEC accession |
| **1988–2007** | Formation of today's large commercial-bank groups and nationwide caja competition | European integration; scale-driven mergers; international expansion/euro |
| **2008–2012** | Systemic restructuring of cajas and creation of new bank vehicles | Real-estate/credit losses; capital and funding pressure; state/EU restructuring |
| **2013–2026** | Absorption of crisis-era groups, EU resolution framework and digital transformation | Post-crisis integration; technology/cost restructuring; strategic M&A and ownership change |

**The interwar expansion was already producing a more concentrated system before the modern merger era.** Banco de España's historical study estimates that deposits expanded roughly twentyfold from 1900 to 1930, while total banking assets rose from around 39% of GDP in 1900 to 69% in 1929. The share of deposits held by the six large banks identified in the study—Hispano Americano, Urquijo, Español de Crédito, Bilbao, Vizcaya and Central—rose from about 47.7% in 1922 to 64.5% in 1934. Registered banks and bankers numbered 104 out of 136 identified institutions in 1925, 126 out of 211 in 1930 and 120 out of 210 in 1935. These figures should be treated as historical-category statistics rather than directly compared with today's credit-institution counts. citeturn5view1

The **Banco de Barcelona failure in 1920** also illustrates an enduring banking-policy problem: liquidity assistance could stop panic without curing insolvency. Banco de España's historical account describes support exceeding 250 million pesetas but an institution that nevertheless failed because its underlying problem was solvency. citeturn5view2

The **Ley de Ordenación Bancaria of December 29, 1921**, associated with Finance Minister Francesc Cambó, helped formalize Banco de España's movement toward a bank-of-banks role and established or reinforced banking institutions and rules concerning capital, liquidity, interest rates and balance-sheet publication. It was an important precursor of modern supervision, although supervision in the contemporary prudential sense emerged only progressively. citeturn5view2turn0search5

The Civil War then broke the institutional continuity of monetary administration. Banco de España's official history records the existence of two issuing institutions and currencies in the opposing zones. After the war, the **1946 Banking Law** replaced the 1921 framework and reinforced an authorization-heavy system in which the state exercised close control over bank creation, expansion and combinations. The law expressly subjected bank mergers and absorptions to public authorization. citeturn0search5turn7search4turn6view1

The decisive break with autarky came with the **1959 Stabilization Plan**. Banco de España's historical work describes the subsequent opening and modernization of the economy, followed by the **1962 credit and banking reform**, which further established Banco de España as the banking system's central institution and gave it broad inspection responsibilities. The reform was modernizing but did not suddenly create a fully liberal banking market; important interventionist features remained. citeturn6view2turn6view3turn7search1

From the late 1970s, savings banks became increasingly direct competitors of commercial banks. The 1977 regulatory reform and later measures progressively reduced restrictions on the cajas. Banco de España's long-run study shows how dramatically their competitive weight increased: the ratio of private-bank deposits to caja deposits fell from about **1.9 in 1980 to 1.4 in 1985 and only slightly above one in 1990**; by the second half of the 1990s the balance had moved in favor of the cajas. citeturn7search0turn5view0

At the same time, Spain suffered a severe **1978–1985 banking crisis**. Institutions such as Banca Catalana, Urquijo, Bankunión, Atlántico, Garriga Nogués and Coca either disappeared as independent entities or moved into larger groups. By the end of 1985, Banesto, Santander, Hispano Americano and Bilbao together represented about **41% of private-bank deposits**. citeturn5view0

Spain's **1986 accession to the European Economic Community** then accelerated a liberalization process that Banco de España dates to the mid-1970s. European harmonization, the Single Market and subsequently monetary union changed the economics of bank scale, funding and cross-border competition. citeturn0search7turn0search17

The long-run story, therefore, is not merely “fewer banks.” It is a transition from **regional and functionally differentiated institutions operating under strong territorial/regulatory constraints** toward nationwide universal banks, followed by European integration, then a crisis that largely removed the caja-as-bank organizational model, and finally a more concentrated system in which physical distribution is shrinking while digital infrastructure becomes more important.

## Institutional genealogies of the surviving groups

The family trees become much clearer when **legal continuity**, **banking-business continuity**, **ownership** and **brand identity** are encoded separately.

| Current group | Historical backbone | Correct interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Santander** | Banco Santander → Central/Hispano lineage → Santander Central Hispano → Santander; Banesto; Popular | Multiple merger/acquisition lineages eventually enter Santander, but Popular entered through resolution and sale, not a normal merger |
| **BBVA** | Banco de Bilbao + Banco de Vizcaya → BBV; Argentaria → BBVA; later Unnim and Catalunya Banc | BBVA combines old private-bank lineages with the former public-bank Argentaria complex |
| **CaixaBank** | Caja de Pensiones + Caja de Barcelona → “la Caixa” → banking business to CaixaBank; Banca Cívica; Bankia/BFA line | The savings-bank/foundation lineage is not legally identical to CaixaBank |
| **Sabadell** | Banco Sabadell plus Herrero, Atlántico, Urquijo, Guipuzcoano, CAM, Banco Gallego and others | Acquisition-led expansion; CAM must retain its intervention/restructuring context |
| **Unicaja** | Five Andalusian cajas → Unicaja; Caja Jaén; CEISS/EspañaDuero; Liberbank | Several distinct caja families converge in the current listed bank |
| **Ibercaja** | 1876 Zaragoza caja → Ibercaja Banco; Caja3 | Caja3 acquisition followed by legal absorption; the original caja became a banking foundation |
| **Kutxabank** | BBK + Kutxa + Vital → banking-business transfers to Kutxabank | The three cajas did not simply “merge away” on January 1, 2012 |
| **ABANCA** | Caixa Galicia + Caixanova → Novacaixagalicia → NCG Banco → sale to Banco Etcheverría/Banesco → ABANCA | A sequence of caja merger, business transfer, state restructuring, ownership sale and rebranding |

**Santander.** Banco Santander itself dates to **1857**. The modern family includes the Banco Central/Banco Hispano Americano lineage, the subsequent Santander Central Hispano combination, Banesto and, later, Banco Popular. Banco de España's map identifies Banesto, Banco Hispano Americano, Banco Popular and numerous other historical banks as ultimately belonging to the Santander lineage. citeturn16search4turn23view1

Banco Popular is the clearest example of why the graph needs mechanism-specific edges. On June 6, 2017, the ECB deemed Popular “failing or likely to fail”; on June 7 the SRB adopted a resolution scheme, wrote down/converted relevant capital instruments and transferred the bank to Santander for **€1**. This should be encoded as `resolution_sale`, not as a conventional acquisition negotiated between two healthy banks. citeturn20search0turn20search4

**BBVA.** Banco de Bilbao was founded in **1857** and Banco de Vizcaya in **1901**. Their boards agreed the combination in 1988, shareholders approved the new Banco Bilbao Vizcaya in June, and the merger became one of the defining early transactions of Spain's modern consolidation era. citeturn16search1turn19search1turn19search11

The second crucial branch is **Argentaria**. BBVA's historical archive identifies Banco Hipotecario de España, Banco Exterior de España, Caja Postal de Ahorros, Banco de Crédito Local, Banco de Crédito Agrícola and Banco de Crédito Industrial among the entities that formed the Argentaria lineage. BBV and Argentaria combined in **1999**, creating present-day BBVA. citeturn19search9turn19search16

The crisis then added **Unnim** and **Catalunya Banc**. BBVA describes Catalunya Banc as its second Spanish acquisition arising from the FROB restructuring process after Unnim, whose integration had been completed in 2013. citeturn16search11

**CaixaBank.** The deeper lineage begins before the project's 1920 timeline. The Caja de Pensiones para la Vejez y de Ahorros was founded in **1904** by Francesc Moragas and civil-society supporters. citeturn21search1turn21search7

In 1990 the modern **Caja de Ahorros y Pensiones de Barcelona** emerged, and in 2011 the group's reorganization transferred the financial business into **CaixaBank**. CaixaBank's own historical material explicitly distinguishes the 1990 caja milestone from the creation of CaixaBank in 2011. citeturn16search13turn16search14

That distinction matters. The appropriate graph is not:

`la Caixa = CaixaBank`

but:

`historical caja → banking-business transfer → CaixaBank`

while the social/institutional ownership lineage subsequently evolves into the banking-foundation/Criteria structure.

Banca Cívica adds another multi-stage path. Its four caja shareholders were **Caja Navarra, Cajasol, CajaCanarias and Caja de Burgos**. CaixaBank's March 2012 agreement explicitly described the operation as a **merger by absorption of Banca Cívica into CaixaBank**, with the four cajas becoming CaixaBank shareholders rather than being themselves directly “merged into CaixaBank” as legal entities. citeturn16search9turn16search10

The Bankia branch is even more important. Banco de España approved the seven-caja SIP involving **Caja Madrid, Bancaja, Caixa Laietana, Caja Insular de Canarias, Caja Ávila, Caja Segovia and Caja Rioja** in June 2010. For visualization purposes, the sequence must preserve **BFA and Bankia as separate nodes**, because the seven-caja integration, the BFA central structure and the transfer of banking activities into Bankia were distinct legal/economic steps. citeturn12search10turn11search5

Bankia was ultimately absorbed by CaixaBank in 2021, creating the final banking-business destination for the seven original cajas as well as the BMN lineage that had previously entered Bankia. CaixaBank's corporate chronology records the Bankia absorption as a separate milestone. citeturn16search14

**Sabadell.** Banco Sabadell's family tree is unusually acquisition-driven. Banco de España's historical map assigns Banco Herrero, Banco Atlántico-related lineages, Banco Urquijo, Banco Guipuzcoano, Banco Gallego and the CAM lineage to Banco Sabadell. citeturn23view2

CAM is a particularly important edge case. Its transition to Sabadell occurred through the crisis-resolution/restructuring machinery, so a simple “CAM merged with Sabadell” label would erase the public intervention and sale process. The website should use a specific `acquisition_after_intervention` or equivalent mechanism rather than a generic merger edge.

Sabadell is also the best contemporary demonstration of the difference between **corporate events and consolidation**. BBVA's takeover offer lapsed on **October 16, 2025**, after acceptances corresponding to only **25.5% of Sabadell voting rights**, below the required threshold. Sabadell therefore did **not** enter the BBVA family tree. citeturn8search5

In October 2026, Groupe BPCE acquired approximately **7%** of Sabadell. Sabadell characterized it as a friendly, long-term strategic investment, and CNMV significant-shareholder information subsequently showed BPCE with **7.000% voting rights**. As of the research cutoff of October 27, 2026, this is a minority-investment edge only—**not an acquisition, parent relationship or change in `current_group`**. citeturn9search0turn9search3turn8search13

**Unicaja.** Unicaja's official history states that the caja was formed in **March 1991** from the savings banks of **Ronda, Cádiz, Almería, Málaga and Antequera**, with Caja de Jaén joining in 2010. Its banking business was segregated to Unicaja Banco in December 2011. citeturn19search2turn19search10

A second lineage runs through **Caja España/Caja Duero → Banco CEISS/EspañaDuero → Unicaja**. Unicaja records the legal absorption of Banco CEISS in September 2018. A third major branch is **Liberbank**, which represented the banking businesses of Cajastur, Caja Extremadura and Caja Cantabria, among other historical components; Unicaja legally absorbed Liberbank on **July 30, 2021**. citeturn17search14turn17search3

**Ibercaja.** The historical caja was founded in Zaragoza in **1876**. Ibercaja's 2026 150th-anniversary documentation confirms that origin and illustrates a rare surviving lineage with very long institutional continuity. citeturn19search0

The modern complication is Caja3. In July 2013 Ibercaja Banco acquired **100% of Banco Grupo Caja3**, whose owners were CAI, Caja Círculo de Burgos and Caja Badajoz; Ibercaja stated at the time that legal absorption of Banco Grupo Caja3 would follow in 2014. citeturn17search1

**Kutxabank.** Kutxabank was legally incorporated in 2011 and began operating on **January 1, 2012**. Its own corporate description is precise: BBK, Kutxa and Caja Vital were the shareholders of the new bank and **transferred their financial businesses** to it. citeturn17search0turn17search7

This should therefore appear as three `banking_business_transfer` relationships, not three merger edges. Banco de España's historical map similarly notes the continued caja/foundation legal lineage after banking moved into Kutxabank. citeturn23view3

**ABANCA.** Banco de España approved the integration of Caixa Galicia and Caixanova in June 2010. The resulting Novacaixagalicia lineage subsequently transferred banking activity into **NCG Banco**. citeturn18search0turn24view3

FROB then sold NCG Banco to **Banco Etcheverría/Grupo Banesco**, with the sale formalized in June 2014, after which the banking group adopted the ABANCA identity. Banco de España's genealogy maps Caixa Galicia, Caixanova and Banco Etcheverría into today's ABANCA line. citeturn18search17turn23view4

Again, this is not one merger. It is better represented as:

**Caixa Galicia + Caixanova → merger → Novacaixagalicia → banking-business transfer → NCG Banco → state restructuring/sale → new ownership → ABANCA branding and integration.**

That level of precision is exactly what the century-scale website needs.

## The crisis restructuring and the modern consolidation endgame

The **2008–2012 period remains the most transaction-dense and legally complex part of the entire history**, and it deserves the highest visual and data resolution.

The crisis interacted with a caja model that had become both geographically expansive and increasingly similar to commercial banking after decades of liberalization. Research published by Banco de España describes the 2009–2011 response as a restructuring dominated by **savings-bank consolidation**, using both conventional mergers/acquisitions and SIPs. citeturn10search8

A SIP should be presented on the website as an **institutional-protection/integration arrangement**, often accompanied by a central bank vehicle and progressively centralized risk, liquidity, solvency and business management. It was not automatically identical to a same-day extinction of the participating cajas.

That difference resolves many of the ambiguities in the original modern dataset:

| Original area of ambiguity | Publication-ready treatment |
|---|---|
| **Caja Madrid / BFA / Bankia** | Keep all three institutional stages. Seven cajas enter the SIP/BFA structure; Bankia is a distinct bank vehicle; Bankia later goes to CaixaBank. citeturn12search10turn11search5 |
| **Cajasol / Banca Cívica** | Cajasol is one of four caja shareholders feeding Banca Cívica; Banca Cívica, not Cajasol itself, is legally absorbed by CaixaBank in 2012. citeturn16search9 |
| **BMN** | Caja Murcia, Caixa Penedès, Sa Nostra and Caja Granada formed the SIP that produced the BMN banking structure; BMN later entered Bankia. citeturn18search5turn18search18 |
| **Caja España / Caja Duero / CEISS** | Preserve caja merger, transfer to Banco CEISS, Unicaja control and eventual legal absorption as separate events. citeturn17search14 |
| **Caja3 / Ibercaja** | CAI, Caja Círculo and Caja Badajoz feed Banco Grupo Caja3; Ibercaja Banco acquired 100% in 2013 and absorbed it subsequently. citeturn17search1 |
| **CAM / Sabadell** | Crisis intervention and sale/acquisition, not a normal voluntary caja merger. |
| **Popular / Santander** | Resolution sale under the EU framework, not ordinary M&A. citeturn20search0 |
| **Catalunya Banc / BBVA** | FROB sale/acquisition after restructuring; BBVA later integrated it. citeturn16search11 |
| **Unnim / BBVA** | Crisis-restructuring acquisition followed by integration. citeturn16search11 |
| **NCG Banco / ABANCA** | State-controlled restructuring, sale to Banco Etcheverría/Banesco and rebranding/integration. citeturn18search17 |
| **BBVA / Sabadell 2025** | Failed acquisition attempt only. No ancestry edge. citeturn8search5 |

The public-assistance numbers also require careful labeling. Banco de España's November 2018 note reported **€54.353 billion of FROB capital support** and **€9.745 billion from the Deposit Guarantee Fund** over the 2009–2017 restructuring period. It reported €5.150 billion recovered at that date and, after considering guarantees and the estimated value of assets still held, calculated net resources of approximately €42.017 billion for FROB and €22.332 billion for the sector-funded deposit-guarantee system. Banco de España explicitly cautioned that this accounting concept was not a comprehensive measure of all economic losses and omitted certain items. The website therefore should not label €54.353 billion, €42.017 billion or any other single figure as simply “the taxpayer cost” without the definition and reference date. citeturn15view3turn18search18

FROB's entity-level data show how unevenly assistance was distributed. Its published table attributes, among other amounts, €22.424 billion of capital instruments to **BFA-Bankia**, €12.052 billion to **Catalunya Banc**, €9.052 billion to **Nova CaixaGalicia**, €5.498 billion to **Banco de Valencia**, €1.645 billion to **BMN**, €977 million to **Banca Cívica**, €407 million to **Caja3** and €124 million to **Liberbank**, under FROB's specified accounting categories. Those values are excellent candidates for an interactive crisis graphic, but the tooltip must state exactly what the column measures. citeturn18search18

SAREB also requires a separate visual grammar. Its role was to receive specified impaired assets from restructuring institutions; it did **not** “absorb” the banks themselves. An institution-to-SAREB relationship therefore belongs in an `asset_transfer` layer that is excluded from family-tree ancestry calculations. citeturn11search5

The post-crisis endgame then moved from emergency restructuring to legal integration. Banking foundations replaced most former cajas as direct bank ownership was separated from their old social/legal form; subsequent combinations such as Bankia–CaixaBank and Liberbank–Unicaja reduced the number of major domestic groups further. Law 26/2013 provided the key legal architecture for the savings-bank/banking-foundation model. citeturn11search10turn21search14

By 2026 the sector is structurally very different from 2008, but not simply “finished consolidating.” Banco de España reports that physical branches have fallen from more than **45,000 in 2008 to fewer than 18,000**, while digital banking has expanded. Its 2025 Financial Inclusion Report nevertheless found that in-person coverage improved between 2021 and end-2024 in some dimensions because alternative channels such as mobile branches expanded. citeturn12search4turn22search15

The sector's current financial condition also contrasts sharply with the crisis years. Banco de España's latest supervisory statistics available before the October 27, 2026 cutoff reported for Q2 2026 a **14.11% CET1 ratio**, **171.07% liquidity coverage ratio**, **2.53% NPL ratio** under the reported definition and **16.05% annualized return on equity** for credit institutions operating in Spain. These are current-condition statistics, not directly comparable with the historical accounting measures used in 1920, 1960 or 1985. citeturn22search0

Strategically, the century-long evidence suggests that **scale has repeatedly become more valuable when the fixed costs of regulation, technology, funding access or distribution rise**, but the history does not prove that larger size automatically guarantees superior efficiency. The cajas themselves are a warning against reducing banking outcomes to size alone: governance, asset concentration, funding structures and risk controls mattered enormously. Banco de España's restructuring research similarly finds differences between merger forms and emphasizes that efficiency/stability effects can unfold differently over time. citeturn10search8

Technology now reinforces some scale economics because core systems, cyber-security, compliance, data and AI investment have substantial fixed-cost components. At the same time, digital distribution lowers the need for a nationwide branch network and therefore can support specialized entrants. The defensible forward-looking scenario is thus **continued strategic pressure for scale alongside room for specialized/digital competitors**, not an unsupported claim that Spain must inevitably move to two or three banks.

## Publication readiness, research gaps and downloadable handoff

The historical extension substantially strengthens the project because it explains **why the institutions that entered the 2008 crisis looked the way they did**, rather than beginning the story only when they started merging.

The most important historical conclusion is that today's structure was produced by several different consolidation regimes:

**pre-war concentration → post-war regulatory protection → developmental expansion → 1970s/1980s crisis and liberalization → European-market megamergers → nationwide caja competition → crisis-driven restructuring → post-crisis legal integration and digitization.**

That sequence explains why a modern family tree cannot be reconstructed safely from bank names alone.

There remain important research gaps. The largest is **not** the backbone of the major modern groups; that is now well supported. The gaps are primarily in exhaustive pre-1988 coverage: exact day-level legal dates for some small regional absorptions, changes in control during the Civil War and early Franco era, and consistent long-run statistics whose accounting definitions do not change across the period. Banco de España's archival guide itself is evidence of the complexity: relevant historical records are distributed across numerous archives and the map was designed to explain genealogies at a closing date rather than to be an exhaustive event-by-event transaction database. citeturn0search0turn24view0

For implementation in Cursor, the safest visualization model is a directed temporal graph in which **node identity never changes merely because a brand changes**, and edge style encodes the legal mechanism. Completed mergers/absorptions may use a solid lineage edge; banking-business transfers need a distinct edge; SIPs should visually converge on a central institution without implying immediate legal extinction; resolution sales should be visibly differentiated; failed bids should be dashed/non-lineage events; minority stakes should belong to an ownership overlay; and SAREB transfers should live in an asset layer outside the institutional family tree.

Temporal filtering should use the most precise supported date and preserve `year`, `announcement_date` and `effective_date` separately. A 2025 BBVA–Sabadell offer should become visible when announced and turn to `failed` on October 16, 2025; it should never alter Sabadell's ancestry. **Publication readiness: Ready with exclusions.**

**Critical unresolved issues: two project-level issues.** First, the original `banks.json` must be mechanically diffed against this supplement before claiming that every original row has been audited. Second, the 1920–1987 period should not be presented as an exhaustive institution-by-institution register; the evidence is strong for the major groups, major failures and regulatory history, but full small-bank coverage would require a substantially larger archival reconstruction.

**The three highest-value remaining research tasks** are to run a deterministic ID-level merge and correction log against the actual existing `banks.json`; deepen the 1920–1987 Banco de España-register genealogy for small and regional predecessors that feed the eight current destination groups; and build a harmonized quantitative series—institutions, branches, employees, deposits/assets and concentration—with explicit definition breaks rather than stitching together incomparable historical categories.

For the website's central question—**“Where did Spain's banks come from, how did they evolve, and how did they become the banking groups we know today?”**—the major historical and institutional backbone is now strong enough to publish, provided the site clearly distinguishes **verified banking-business lineage from legal identity, ownership, branding and broader historical context**.
